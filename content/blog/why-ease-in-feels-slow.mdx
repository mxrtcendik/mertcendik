`ease-in` starts slowly and then rushes to catch up. People judge a transition by its first few frames, so that slow start is most of what they notice. A dropdown with `ease-in` at 300ms feels heavier than the same dropdown with ease-out at 300ms. The duration is the same. The slow part has just moved to the moment people are watching.

I don't use `ease-in` for interface motion at all. What I use instead depends on what's moving:

- **Something entering or leaving:** ease-out. It responds right away, then settles.
- **Something already on screen that moves or changes shape:** ease-in-out, so it speeds up and slows down instead of slamming into the end.
- **A hover or a color change:** `ease`.
- **Constant motion, like a marquee or a progress fill:** `linear`.

The built-in curves are too gentle for UI, so I keep three of my own:

```css
:root {
  --ease-out: cubic-bezier(0.23, 1, 0.32, 1);
  --ease-in-out: cubic-bezier(0.77, 0, 0.175, 1);
  --ease-drawer: cubic-bezier(0.32, 0.72, 0, 1);
}
```

`--ease-drawer` is the sheet curve from Ionic. It comes in fast and then settles, which is exactly what a panel sliding up from the bottom should do. I'd rather use a curve someone has already tuned than draw one that looks great on a graph and wrong in the product. [easing.dev](https://easing.dev) and [easings.co](https://easings.co) are where I look before making my own.

Duration is the other half, and I almost always end up shorter than my first guess. A press is 100–160ms. A tooltip or small popover is 125–200ms. A menu is 150–250ms. A dialog or drawer is 200–500ms, and only the ones that travel far get the upper end. A menu at 180ms feels much quicker than the same menu at 400ms, even though it's doing exactly the same thing.

Enter and exit don't have to take the same time. The [hold-to-confirm button](/blog/revealing-with-clip-path) fills over about two seconds on `linear` while you decide, then snaps back in 200ms if you let go. It's slow while the person is deciding, and fast once the interface is responding.

Springs solve a different problem: drags, gestures you can reverse halfway, anything that should keep its momentum when it's interrupted. A CSS keyframe animation starts over from zero when you interrupt it. A transition can change its target. A spring keeps its current speed and settles from there, which is why a drawer you flick closed feels right on a spring.

I still wouldn't use a spring on a menu opened from the keyboard. [How often someone sees it](/blog/should-this-animate) matters more than the curve. Even a good curve is a delay when you trigger it all day.

When a transition feels off and I can't tell why, I slow it down to four or five times its duration, or step through it in the browser's Animations panel. At full speed, a wrong origin, a crossfade that shows two objects at once, or a curve that stalls at the start are easy to miss. Slowed down, they're obvious. I fix it there, then put the duration back.
